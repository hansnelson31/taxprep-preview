01
Real Estate Professional Status

Put suspended rental losses to work now

Rental real estate losses are normally "passive" — stuck offsetting only passive income, piling up unused year after year. Real estate professional status (REPS) changes that.

{% include icons/home.html %}

Who qualifies

Generally, more than half your working hours and at least 750 hours a year need to be spent in a real property trade or business — development, construction, property management, brokerage, or rental operations count.

{% include icons/hard-hat.html %}

Why it's a natural fit for the trades

If you're already a contractor, handyman, fence builder, or anyone else working in a real property trade or business, you're halfway to qualifying already. Put a rental into service and you're already a step ahead — your losses aren't locked up like everyone else's.

02
Short-Term Rentals

The other way in, without REPS

Short-term rentals — average guest stay of 7 days or less, or 30 days or less with meaningful services provided — aren't automatically treated as passive rental activity in the first place. Materially participate in running it, and losses can offset your other active income directly, no real estate professional status required.

03
Partnerships, GPs & LPs

Structure matters as much as strategy

{% include icons/users.html %}

Allocating income & losses

Partnerships can allocate profit, loss, and specific deductions differently among partners — useful when partners bring different capital, labor, or risk to the table.

{% include icons/landmark.html %}

Debt & basis

How debt is classified — recourse or nonrecourse — changes how much loss each partner can actually deduct. Getting this wrong is one of the most common ways real estate losses get disallowed.

{% include icons/file-percent.html %}

GP vs. LP

General partner or limited partner status affects self-employment tax exposure and how actively you can be involved without changing your tax treatment.

04
Cost Segregation

Accelerate depreciation on what you already own

A cost segregation study reclassifies parts of a building — fixtures, flooring, land improvements — out of 27.5- or 39-year depreciation and into 5-, 7-, or 15-year categories instead. We don't perform the engineering study ourselves, but we know how to implement the results and coordinate with a cost-seg firm to make sure the deduction actually lands where it should on your return.

05
1031 & "Lazy" 1031 Exchanges

Deferring gains, two different ways

{% include icons/refresh-cw.html %}

Traditional 1031 exchange

Swap into like-kind real estate and defer the gain from the property you sold — the classic route, with strict identification and closing deadlines to manage.

{% include icons/refresh-cw.html %}

The "lazy" 1031

Instead of a formal exchange, simply buy new real estate and run a cost segregation study on it — the resulting bonus depreciation can offset the gain from your sale, without the exchange's timelines or restrictions. With 100% bonus depreciation now available for qualified improvements, leasehold improvements, land improvements, and the short-life components a cost seg study identifies, this has become a genuinely powerful alternative.
